How the check works

It is the check your bank already runs on your card, moved in front of a payment that cannot be undone.

It is a fraud check, not a blocklist

When your card is declined on a strange purchase, your bank is not consulting a register of bad shops. It is asking whether this purchase looks like you: wrong city, wrong hour, far larger than usual, following a run of small test charges. The shop is often perfectly legitimate and the payment is stopped anyway, because the evidence is in the pattern rather than in the counterparty's reputation.

Crypto risk tools mostly do the opposite. They ask whether the receiving address appears on a list of known-bad addresses. That answers well when the address is known — and the entire difficulty is that it is not. The address collecting a scam is typically weeks old and has never been reported, because the people paying it have not yet realised anything is wrong. By the time it is listed, it has been emptied.

There is one more difference, and it is the one that sets where the check has to sit. Your bank can reverse a card payment. Nobody can reverse a stablecoin transfer — not us, not the issuer, not the chain. So the check has to happen in the seconds before you press send, which is why this is a page you use beforehand rather than a report you read afterwards.

What it reads

Two questions, and the second one only when a wallet is connected.

What does this address do with the money people send it? Seventeen numbers, all computed from the address's own transfer history, read live off the chain when you paste it: how many unrelated wallets have ever paid it and how many in the last week, how long money sits before it moves again, what share of what arrives leaves again, how many different addresses it pays out to, how much each payer sends before stopping, and how long it has existed.

Does this transfer fit your history with it? Thirty-seven numbers when your wallet is connected, adding the relationship: whether you have paid this address before, how this amount compares with your last transfer to it and with your own typical transfer, whether you and the address share any counterparty, and how much of your balance is moving.

The shape being looked for is specific. Money arrives from many wallets that each pay once and never return, sits for minutes, and leaves to one or two destinations. The difficulty is that a perfectly ordinary exchange deposit address looks like that too, which is why the answer comes from seventeen numbers weighed together rather than from any one of them.

Collection point

  • Many payers, each once. Hundreds of unrelated wallets, almost none returning.
  • Minutes, not days. Money is forwarded almost as fast as it lands.
  • Swept, not spent. Everything leaves to one or two addresses.
  • Young. Often weeks old, with no history before the scam started.

Exchange deposit address

  • Many payers, each once. Identical, and completely innocent.
  • Minutes, not days. Also identical — deposits are swept on a schedule.
  • Swept to a hot wallet. One destination, every time.
  • Old, and steady. Years of the same behaviour at a constant rate.

Three of the four signals match. That is the real problem, and it is why a rule like “warn when an address forwards everything it receives” is useless: three quarters of ordinary addresses forward everything they receive, because a wallet that is paid and then spends has done exactly that.

Where the right answers come from

Tether's USDT contract emits an AddedBlackList event on-chain when it freezes an address, normally at law-enforcement request. Those events are public, timestamped by the block they sit in, and produced by somebody other than us. They are the answer key the model is trained and graded against.

They are deliberately not consulted when you check an address. That is the point: by the time an address is on the list, the money has gone. The list is useful for telling us afterwards whether we were right, and useless for telling you now whether to send.

Every feature is computed only from transfers that existed before the moment being scored. A model that has seen an address's later behaviour can appear to predict a freeze it was in fact told about, and the result is a backtest that cannot be reproduced in production. Keeping that line clean is most of the work.

Read as one sentence: at roughly one stop per hundred ordinary payments, 97% of the money heading into a collection point meets something. Every one of those cases is listed individually, including the misses and the false alarms, on previous checks.

Timing matters more than the rate. The scores are as good three months before a freeze as they are the day before it — tested against 163 addresses frozen after the training data was cut, so the model had never encountered any of them.

It runs in your browser

The model is a gradient-boosted ensemble of decision trees: 400 of them for the address check, 300 for the two-sided one. They are shipped to the page as JSON and walked in JavaScript, so the scoring happens on your machine. The address you type is sent to a public block explorer, because that is where the transaction history lives. It is not sent to us. We have no account, no login, and no record of what anyone checked.

The same trees are walked in Python for everything measured on this site. The two implementations are held to each other on every build: identical verdicts, and scores agreeing to within 2.2e-16 — floating point's own resolution. A model that quietly behaves differently in the browser than in the evaluation would make every number here a statement about a program nobody runs.

Published before the fact

Each night the system samples addresses receiving USDT right now, drops any already on a list, scores the rest, and writes down the ones it calls high-risk — with the score, the features behind it, and the model file's own checksum. The entries are hash-chained, so an earlier one cannot be changed without breaking every entry after it, and they are committed by a scheduled job on GitHub, whose timestamps we do not control.

That record is the only evidence that settles the question, and it can only be earned by waiting.

What it does not do

  • —It cannot stop or reverse anything. It is a reading, taken before you act, and the decision stays yours.
  • —It does not know who owns an address. There is no identity here, no KYC, and no personal data — only public transaction history.
  • —An address with no history cannot be scored. A wallet created an hour ago has told us nothing yet.
  • —USDT on Tron and Ethereum. Other chains and tokens are not covered, and the page says so rather than guessing.
  • —“Nothing we would stop you for” means we found no case against the address. It is a statement about our evidence, not a guarantee about your money.
