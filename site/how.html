How the check works

Your bank checks card payments for fraud before they go through. This does the same job for stablecoin transfers.

A fraud check, not a blocklist

When your card is declined on a strange purchase, your bank is not consulting a register of bad shops. It asks whether this purchase looks like you: wrong city, wrong hour, far bigger than usual, right after a run of small test charges. The shop is often perfectly legitimate and the payment is stopped anyway.

Most crypto risk tools work the other way. They ask whether the receiving address is on a list of known-bad addresses. That answer comes too late, and here is why.

  1. Day 0 The address is created. It has no history, and there is nothing yet for anyone to see.
  2. Week 3 or so Strangers start paying it, each one once, and the money leaves within minutes. There is already enough history here to tell what it is. This is where the check runs. We can answer here
  3. Soon after The money is swept onward and gone. No one can reverse a stablecoin transfer: not us, not the issuer, not the chain.
  4. Months later Tether freezes the address, usually at law-enforcement request, and it lands on every list at once. In our held-out cases the typical gap was 195 days. A blocklist answers here

Why it is hard

Start with the obvious signals. A scam collection point and a busy legitimate address, such as a shared exchange deposit address or a payment processor, are not distinguishable on any of them.

So it is not age, and it is certainly not "money left quickly". The strongest single signal here is how much each wallet sends before it stops paying, which is the difference between somebody moving their savings and somebody topping up an account. Those groups are 91% and 10% later frozen respectively, so the split is real and not an artefact of the model's own opinion.

No row decides it on its own. The model gets payer counts at one day, seven days, thirty days and ever, and dollar totals at seven and thirty days, so it sees the rate and whether the rate is changing rather than a lifetime count. It gets two quantiles of holding time rather than one, so an address that sweeps some deposits instantly and lets others sit looks different from one that treats everything the same. Seventeen numbers, 400 trees, and the interactions between them are the model.

This is also why a hand-written rule fails. "Warn when an address forwards everything it receives" fires on three quarters of ordinary addresses, because a wallet that gets paid and then spends has forwarded everything it received.

What happens when you press Send

  1. You give an address and an amount. nothing is sent, and nothing is signed
  2. Your browser reads that address's whole transfer history from a public block explorer. often more than 1,000 transfers, a few seconds
  3. It turns that history into 17 numbers: how many strangers pay it, how fast money leaves, how much it keeps, how many places it pays out to, how old it is. 37 numbers when a wallet is connected, adding your relationship to it and the amount
  4. 400 decision trees vote on those numbers, and the score lands in one of three bands. the trees run on your machine, not ours

The bands are not round numbers. "High" is set where only the top 1% of ordinary wallets land. A stop means the address behaves unlike 99 out of 100 of the wallets people pay every day.

That is the number that matters: 96.7% of the money moving into an address that later turned out to be a scam ran into something first.

It is not free. The same check also looked at 3,484 transfers to addresses that were never listed, and stopped 1.1% of them while warning on another 12%. So about one ordinary payment in ninety gets a stop it did not need. That is the price of the figure above, and the reason the threshold sits where it does rather than lower.

Every one of those cases is listed individually, misses and false alarms included, under previous checks.

Timing matters more than the rate. The scores are as good three months before a freeze as the day before it, tested against 163 addresses frozen after the training data was cut, none of which the model had ever encountered.

Where the right answers come from

Tether's USDT contract emits an AddedBlackList event on-chain when it freezes an address. Those events are public, timestamped by the block they sit in, and produced by somebody other than us. They are the answer key.

We never consult that list when you check an address. By the time a freeze happens the money has gone. The list can tell us afterwards whether we were right. It cannot tell you now whether to send.

Every number is computed only from transfers that existed before the moment being scored. A model that has seen an address's later behaviour can look like it predicted a freeze it was actually told about. The backtest then scores well and the live system does not. Most of the work goes into enforcing that rule.

It runs in your browser

The trees ship to the page as JSON and are walked in JavaScript, so the scoring happens on your machine. The address you type goes to a public block explorer, because that is where transaction history lives. It does not come to us. There is no account, no login, and no record of what anyone checked.

The same trees are run in Python for everything measured here. Every build checks the two against each other: same verdicts, and scores matching to 2.2e-16, the smallest gap a computer can represent. If the browser scored differently from the version we test, the numbers on this page would not describe what you actually get.

Published before the fact

Each night the system samples addresses receiving USDT right now, drops any already on a list, scores the rest, and writes down the ones it calls high-risk, along with the score, the features behind it, and the model file's checksum.

The entries are hash-chained, so changing an earlier one breaks every entry after it, and a scheduled job on GitHub commits them with timestamps we do not control.

Only time can show whether those calls were right.

What it does not do

  • It cannot stop or reverse anything. It gives you a reading before you act. The decision is yours.
  • It does not know who owns an address. No identity, no KYC, no personal data. Only public transaction history.
  • An address with no history cannot be scored. A wallet created an hour ago has told us nothing yet.
  • USDT on Tron and Ethereum only. Other chains and tokens are not covered, and the page says so rather than guessing.
  • "Nothing we would stop you for" means we found no case against the address. That is a statement about our evidence, not a guarantee about your money.

Every figure here is regenerated from the code that produces it, and every address can be checked on Tronscan or Etherscan. You can also look up any address on its own, with the full reasoning and the prediction record behind it.
